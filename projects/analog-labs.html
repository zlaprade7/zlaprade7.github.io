Analog electronicsWhere does the noise come in.

Differential pairs and CMRR, cascodes, MOSFET and op-amp behaviour, push-pull output stages. Simulated in LTspice, then built and measured.

The useful part wasn't the topologies. It was getting into the habit of asking, every time: where does the noise come in, and what rejects it? That question is why the water bottle has an instrumentation amp instead of a bare ADC read, and why the bench instrument's MOSFETs sit inside the feedback loop.

Sensors and transducersA number without an error bar is a guess.

One lab: measure a potentiometer's shaft angle by making it set the frequency of a 555 astable, then read that frequency on an ESP32 with interrupt-based period averaging. Resistance becomes frequency, frequency becomes a timestamp difference, and that becomes an angle.

The transfer function falls out of the 555's internals. The capacitor always charges and discharges through exactly ln(2) time constants, because the internal comparators trip at a third and two thirds of Vcc. That's where the famous 1.44 comes from. It isn't magic, it's 1/ln(2).

The part that actually taught me something was the uncertainty analysis. Propagating component tolerances through with RSS partial derivatives showed that the capacitor dominates the error budget, and that total uncertainty isn't constant across the range. It runs from about 1.7% at one end to 4% at the other, because sensitivity goes as 1/f². The measurement is meaningfully worse at one end of the sweep, and you'd never know that from the schematic.

That's the thing I took out of the whole course. A number without an error bar isn't a measurement, it's a guess with a decimal point.
