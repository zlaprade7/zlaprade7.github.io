ELEE 4270Analog electronics.

Differential pairs and CMRR, cascode stages, MOSFET and op-amp behaviour, push-pull output stages — simulated in LTspice, then built and measured.

The useful part wasn't the topologies. It was the habit of asking, every time, where does the noise come in, and what rejects it? That question is the reason the water bottle has an instrumentation amplifier instead of a bare ADC read, and it's why the bench instrument's MOSFETs sit inside the feedback loop.

ELEE 4230Sensors and transducers.

The course that taught me that a sensor reading is a claim, and a claim needs an error bar.

Encoding angle as frequency

One lab: measure a potentiometer's shaft angle by making it set the frequency of a 555 astable oscillator, then reading that frequency on an ESP32 via interrupt-based period averaging. Resistance becomes frequency; frequency becomes a timestamp difference; a timestamp difference becomes an angle.

The transfer function falls out of the 555's internals — the capacitor always charges and discharges through exactly ln(2) time constants, because the internal comparators trip at ⅓ and ⅔ of VCC. That's where the famous 1.44 comes from. It isn't a magic number; it's 1/ln(2).

The part that actually taught me something: the uncertainty analysis. Propagating component tolerances through with RSS partial derivatives showed that the capacitor dominates the error budget — and that the total uncertainty isn't constant across the range. It runs from roughly 1.7% at one end to 4% at the other, because sensitivity goes as 1/f². The measurement is meaningfully worse at one end of the sweep than the other, and you'd never know it from the schematic.

That's the lesson I took out of the whole course: a number without an error bar isn't a measurement, it's a guess with a decimal point.
