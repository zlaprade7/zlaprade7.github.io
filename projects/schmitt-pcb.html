Schmitt Trigger PCB

Five revisions of the same circuit over one summer, for no class and no credit. The Schmitt trigger was never the point.

Role
Solo, self-directed
Tools
Altium, Fusion 360
Revisions
5
Assigned by
Nobody

WhyNobody was going to teach me Altium.

No course required this. I'd done plenty of circuit analysis on paper and in simulation, and none of it ever made me answer the question a fab house asks, which is whether the thing can actually be built.

So over a summer I taught myself Altium and Fusion 360 from nothing, and picked a circuit simple enough that the circuit wouldn't be the hard part.

The circuitPositive feedback, deliberately.

A Schmitt trigger is a comparator with hysteresis, so it has two thresholds instead of one. You build it by feeding the output back to the non-inverting input, which is the opposite of what you spend a whole analog course learning to do.

That positive feedback is the trick. Once the output starts moving it drives itself further the same way, which snaps the transition and shifts the threshold away from the input. The result is a comparator that won't chatter on a noisy or slow-moving signal, because the input has to travel a real distance to flip it back.

Five revisionsWhat actually changed.

The schematic barely moved across the five boards. Nearly everything I learned was in the layout. Footprints that didn't match real parts, routing that passed DRC and would still have been miserable to assemble, and the slow realisation that a board is a mechanical object as much as an electrical one.

OutcomeThe skill, not the board.

The board itself is unremarkable and I'd say so in an interview. What it bought me was fluency: schematic capture, footprint libraries, layout, DRC, Gerber export. The boring pipeline that sits between a working circuit and a physical object.

Every board I've designed since started from what those five taught me.
