Schmitt Trigger PCB

Five Altium revisions of the same circuit over one summer, for no course and no credit. The circuit was chosen precisely because it was not the hard part.

Role
Solo, self-directed
Tools
Altium Designer, Fusion 360
Revisions
5
Assigned by
Nobody

MotivationNothing in my coursework was going to teach me Altium.

No course required this. I had done a great deal of circuit analysis on paper and in simulation, and none of it had ever forced me to answer the question a fabrication house asks, which is whether the design can actually be manufactured.

Over a summer I taught myself Altium Designer and Fusion 360 from nothing, and deliberately selected a circuit simple enough that the circuit would not be where the difficulty lay.

CircuitPositive feedback, used deliberately.

A Schmitt trigger is a comparator with hysteresis: two thresholds rather than one. It is built by feeding the output back to the non-inverting input, which is the opposite of the negative feedback an entire analog course is spent teaching.

The positive feedback is the mechanism. Once the output begins moving it drives itself further in the same direction, which snaps the transition and shifts the threshold away from the input. The result is a comparator that does not chatter on a noisy or slow-moving signal, because the input must travel a real distance to flip it back.

Five revisionsWhat actually changed.

The schematic barely moved across the five boards. Nearly everything I learned was in the layout: footprints that did not match real parts, routing that passed DRC and would still have been miserable to assemble, and the gradual realisation that a board is a mechanical object as much as an electrical one.

[This section needs the revision history.] One line per revision: what was wrong with the previous board and what changed. That list is the substance of this project. Five iterations of one circuit only carries weight if each iteration has a stated reason.

OutcomeThe pipeline, not the board.

The board itself is unremarkable, and I would say so in an interview. What it produced was fluency in the pipeline that sits between a working circuit and a physical object: schematic capture, footprint libraries, layout, DRC, Gerber export.

Every board I have designed since, including the Hydration Coach front end and the bench instrument, started from what these five taught me.
