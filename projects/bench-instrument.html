Raspberry Pi Bench Instrument

Function generator, voltmeter, ohmmeter and DC reference in one box, driven by a rotary encoder and an LCD. I was project lead and electrical lead on a team of five, and I designed the analog side.

The ruleBuild the instruments, don't buy them.

The constraint that made this worth doing: no instrumentation ICs. No ADC chip. No ohmmeter front end. If the box needed to measure a voltage, we had to build the thing that measures voltage.

That turns a software project into an analog one. A Raspberry Pi has no analog input at all, so the voltmeter isn't a library call. It's a circuit you have to invent.

I ran the project and owned the analog side. What follows is the part I designed.

VoltmeterAn ADC made from resistors and a comparator.

The Pi can't read an analog voltage, so I built a 6-bit successive approximation ADC out of parts.

An R-2R ladder across six GPIO pins makes a trial voltage. A comparator compares that trial to the unknown input and gives back one bit: higher or lower. The Pi walks down from the most significant bit, keeping each one that doesn't overshoot. Six comparisons, six bits, one conversion. A binary search, done in hardware.

This is the thing I'd want to be asked about. Almost everyone grabs an ADS1115 and moves on. Building the converter meant I had to understand what an ADC actually is: the ladder's output impedance, the comparator's offset and delay, the settling time you have to respect between trials, and why 6 bits was the honest limit rather than a number I picked. There's no datasheet doing that work for you.

OhmmeterSweep a divider until it trips.

Same rule, different trick. The unknown resistor sits in the top leg of a voltage divider. The bottom leg is an MCP4231 digital pot in series with a fixed resistor. The Pi sweeps the pot through its 128 steps and watches a comparator. Whichever step flips it tells you the unknown value.

The real design work was picking that fixed resistor, because it decides where the measurable window sits. I started at 324Ω and moved to 422Ω to centre the range. That put the floor around 526Ω and the ceiling near 9.8kΩ, which covers 500Ω to 10k properly instead of wasting half the pot's travel below anything I cared about.

Push-pull ampDriving a real load off one supply.

The output stage is a Class AB push-pull. A TL071CP op-amp drives complementary MOSFETs, an IRFZ24N sourcing current and an IRF5210 sinking it.

We only had a single 24V rail, so I split it into ±12V with a resistor divider, Zener clamps and a bypass cap to make a stable virtual ground. Not as clean as a true dual supply, but it's what the bench gave us and the Zener holds the mid-rail steady enough.

The bit worth defending

The MOSFETs sit inside the op-amp's feedback loop. That's what kills crossover distortion, the dead zone where neither device is conducting. Feedback forces the op-amp to swing hard through that region to keep the output tracking, so the loop hides the nonlinearity the output stage would otherwise show. A 100Ω gate resistor limits gate current and keeps it from oscillating.

Three bugsThings the bench caught that simulation didn't.

If I were hiring someone, this is the section I'd read first.

  • The coefficients drifted apart. Converting resistance to step and step back to resistance used two different pairs of coefficients, for what should have been inverse operations. The ohmmeter read plausibly wrong, which is the worst kind of wrong. Fixed by making one source of truth.
  • Floor and ceiling were backwards. When the comparator saturates you're out of range, but the two saturation cases were swapped and the boundaries were off by about 200Ω. Nothing in the code looked incorrect. Only testing against known resistors found it.
  • Step zero wasn't zero volts. The DC reference's clear routine wrote step 0 to the digipot, which maps to -5V, not 0V. It should have written step 64. I'd assumed a midpoint was a minimum.

All three have the same shape. The code was self-consistent and the reasoning behind it was wrong. Simulation can't catch that, which is the whole argument for building the thing.
