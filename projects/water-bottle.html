Instrumented Water Bottle

Five sensors, a custom analog front end, a two-layer board and a printed housing. Designed, fabricated and debugged end to end.

Role
Solo. Hardware, firmware, CAD.
Timeline
10 weeks
Board
2-layer, EasyEDA / JLCPCB
Status
V1 built. V2 in progress.

WhyI get migraines and they're tied to dehydration.

That's the whole reason this exists. Not a hypothetical user or a market gap. A problem I have, that I can measure, and that hardware might be able to help with.

If I drink enough water I get fewer migraines. The trouble is I don't notice I've stopped drinking until it's already too late. So: a bottle that notices for me.

Building it properly meant dealing with the fact that every sensor wants a different kind of care. A thermistor is a resistance, not a voltage, so it needs a bridge and a gain stage before an ADC can do anything with it. An ultrasonic sensor inside a bottle has to cope with reflections, condensation and a surface that moves whenever you do. An IMU gives you acceleration, and "a sip happened" is an inference, not a reading.

Analog front endGetting a usable signal out of a thermistor.

A thermistor's resistance change is small, and reading it straight into the ESP32's ADC throws away most of the resolution. So I put it in one leg of a Wheatstone bridge. The output is then a differential voltage that sits near zero at balance and swings with temperature.

That goes into an INA121P instrumentation amp at a gain of 2. High input impedance, high CMRR, gain set by one external resistor. The in-amp rejects the common-mode noise the bridge picks up and hands the ADC something that actually uses its range.

The thermistor is a Semitec 103AT-2, and its resistance-to-temperature curve is famously nonlinear. Rather than fake it with a straight line I linearise it with the Steinhart-Hart equation. Three coefficients, good to a fraction of a degree across the range I care about.

When the design moved to a board, the INA121P became an INA333. Same job, surface-mount package. Prototype with the part you can hold, ship the part that fits the board.

The sensor I didn't useCapacitive rods would have been easier.

The obvious way to measure water level is capacitive: two electrodes in the liquid, read the capacitance. Cheap, linear, and it's what most designs reach for.

I turned it down. The rods have to sit in the water I'm going to drink, in a bottle I have to clean. So I used an A02YYUW ultrasonic instead, which measures the surface from above and touches nothing.

It's a harder sensor to work with. Reflections, condensation, outlier spikes that need a median filter to kill. I picked it anyway, because a measurement that compromises the thing being measured isn't worth having.

This is the tradeoff I'd want to be asked about. The easy sensor was the wrong sensor. Picking the harder one and then doing the filtering work to make it behave is the actual engineering.

The rest are digital and better behaved. An SHT40 for ambient conditions over I²C, and an MPU-6050 for orientation and sip detection. Each one gets a filter matched to how it fails: Kalman on the noisy analog channel, median to kill the ultrasonic's spikes, EMA for slow drift. One filter for all five would have been the lazy answer and a worse one.

PrototypeMeasure the circuit, then design around it.

Before any CAD I built the whole chain on a breadboard and got it working, then put calipers on it and measured the footprint.

The housing was designed around the real dimensions of a circuit I'd already proven, not a shape I hoped the electronics would fit into. Doing it the other way round is how you end up with a nice printed part and a board that won't go in it.

PowerThe part nobody looks at.

The sensors get the attention, but the power path is where a portable device lives or dies. Mine is a complete chain and every link is there for a reason.

  • USB-C in through a USB4105-GF-A, with 5.1kΩ CC pull-downs. Without those a USB-C host won't deliver current at all. It's the most common mistake on a first USB-C board.
  • USBLC6-2SC6 for ESD protection on the data lines. A port a human touches will eventually get zapped.
  • TP4056 for LiPo charge management.
  • DW01A and FS8205A for battery protection: over-charge, over-discharge, over-current. A lithium cell in a bottle I carry around isn't a place to skip that.
  • AP2112K-3.3 regulating down to the 3.3V rail.

Most student boards are a microcontroller with a USB cable hanging off them. None of this is glamorous and all of it is what makes the difference between a demo and a device.

V2Make it cheaper and make it fit any bottle.

V1 works. It's also expensive, complicated, and welded to one specific bottle, which quietly defeats the point. I wanted something I'd actually use every day, and a bespoke instrumented bottle isn't that. It's a demo.

So I'm rebuilding it as something closer to a keychain hydration counter. Small, cheap, works with whatever bottle I'm carrying.

The scope cut is the point. Five sensors was a good exercise and a bad product. Nearly all the value sits in one question, which is whether I've had enough water today, and answering that doesn't need a Wheatstone bridge and an ultrasonic rangefinder. Stripping it back to the sensor that earns its place is harder than adding four more.

What carries over: motion-based sip detection. The IMU was always doing the real work. What might not survive: the analog front end. It's the part I'm proudest of and it may not make the cost target, and being able to say that out loud is part of the job.
