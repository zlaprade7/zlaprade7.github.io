Hydration Coach

A water bottle that measures how much you drink, the conditions you're drinking in, and how much you're moving, then adjusts your daily target accordingly. Five sensors, a custom analog front end, a two-layer board, and a printed housing, built to a $50 cap.

The problemA hydration problem I actually have.

When I get busy with classes and commitments I stop drinking water. The result is fatigue, brain fog, and eventually migraines that can last more than a day. This has been a problem for about six years, and I've tried a number of solutions: a smart Gatorade bottle, phone apps, and asking my parents to remind me.

Each one failed for a specific reason. The Gatorade bottle was too loud to carry daily. The apps used hardcoded targets that were either too much or too little on any given day. And I didn't want to make my hydration someone else's job.

So the design brief was to take the parts that worked, water-level detection and a reminder that reaches my phone, and fix the part that didn't: the fixed goal. A person needs roughly a gallon of water a day, but temperature, humidity and activity all shift that number. Those are the parameters this project measures.

Sensor selectionThree measurements, and one sensor I turned down.

Hydration here is inferred from water level, the ambient environment, and user activity. Each needed a sensor, and each choice was a tradeoff.

Water level: A02YYUW waterproof ultrasonic

The obvious alternative was capacitive rods, at roughly the same price. I rejected them. Capacitive sensing is intrusive, since the electrodes sit in the water I'm going to drink and clean, and it requires calibration. Worse, capacitance shifts with water temperature, which would force the user to drink water at a specific temperature for an accurate reading, and a metal bottle changes the capacitance again.

The ultrasonic measures the surface from above and never touches the water. It is not temperature dependent. It is more expensive at $17.88 and it carries its own error sources, including air purity and altitude affecting the speed of sound, but those are smaller and more predictable than the ones capacitive sensing introduces.

The easy sensor was the wrong sensor. Capacitive rods were cheaper and simpler, and they would have compromised the thing being measured. Choosing the harder sensor and then doing the filtering work to make it behave is the part of this project I would defend first.

Temperature: Semitec 103AT-2 thermistor and SHT40

Sensor fusion was a project requirement, so temperature is measured twice: once with a raw NTC thermistor and once with a packaged IC. The 103AT-2 is small, has low uncertainty, and comes from a reliable manufacturer. The SHT40 is accurate to ±0.2°C and gives humidity as well, which matters because higher humidity increases dehydration.

Motion: MPU-6050

The L3GD20H is more accurate but more expensive and has only one IMU. Precision isn't critical here. All I need is a broad tilt measurement to detect a drinking event and a rough velocity for activity, so the cheaper part was the correct call and it kept the build under budget.

Analog front endWheatstone bridge and an instrumentation amplifier.

The thermistor's raw output through a plain voltage divider is nearly flat across the temperature range that matters. Reading it straight into the ESP32's ADC throws away most of the available resolution, so the signal has to be amplified before it is digitised.

The thermistor sits in one leg of a Wheatstone bridge: a 10kΩ thermistor against a 10kΩ resistor, with a matched 10k/10k divider forming the reference leg at 1.65V. The differential output goes into an INA333 instrumentation amplifier at a gain of 3.

An instrumentation amplifier was the right choice over an op-amp plus RC filter because of its high CMRR, which is what cleans the signal while amplifying it. Getting comparable common-mode rejection from a discrete op-amp would require very tightly matched resistors to hit a figure the INA333 guarantees out of the box.

The low-pass filter earned its place

A capacitor sits in parallel with the bridge output node. Without it, moving the circuit or bringing other electronics near it produced spikes of about 0.06V. With it, that dropped to a periodic 0.01V. The thermistor signal is DC, so a low-pass filter removes high-frequency noise from surrounding electronics while leaving the signal untouched. It is cheap and it measurably worked.

LinearisationWhy I used the beta model and not Steinhart-Hart.

A thermistor's resistance-to-temperature curve is exponential, so converting voltage back to a temperature needs a mathematical model. I started with the Steinhart-Hart equation, which is accurate to about 0.01°C, and then deliberately moved away from it.

I chose the beta model instead. It needs two parameters, both given on the datasheet: β = 3435 and the resistance at 25°C. The manufacturer has already tested and confirmed those values, and the model is accurate to under 1°C across 0°C to 100°C, which is well inside what this project needs.

The reasoning is a systems argument, not an accuracy one. The beta model's math is simpler, so the ESP32 does less work, so the board draws less power, so there is less heat inside the housing, and the housing's temperature is the thing that most threatens the accuracy of an ambient temperature measurement. Chasing 0.01°C with Steinhart-Hart would have cost accuracy elsewhere. And I already have the SHT40 at ±0.2°C to fuse against, so the second decimal place on the thermistor buys nothing.

The three-point method was also considered and rejected, since the curve is exponential and a three-point fit would carry large error. The beta model has known drawbacks, including drift over long periods, but the datasheet range of -50°C to 110°C comfortably covers the 0°C to 100°C water range this project assumes.

FilteringA different filter for each sensor.

Each sensor fails differently, so each one gets a filter matched to how it fails. Using one filter for all of them would have been the easier answer and a worse one.

  • Thermistor and SHT40: Kalman. The filter predicts the next value from past values, so isolated spikes get averaged out. Room temperature does not physically jump, and on the rare occasion it does, such as carrying the bottle from a cold building into summer heat, the Kalman filter converges on the new true value after a short time rather than rejecting it.
  • Ultrasonic: median. A Kalman filter is the wrong tool here, because the ultrasonic only fires on motion or tilt, so there is no continuous series to predict from. Instead the filter takes five measurements and reports the median, which discards outlier spikes without needing a model.
  • MPU-6050: no library. I averaged the directional tilts and velocities into a single magnitude, which was stable enough to detect movement and a tilt past the drinking threshold.

Tilt detection

Drinking is detected geometrically. The gravity magnitude is |g| = √(ax² + ay² + az²), and the tilt angle is θ = arccos(az / |g|). A consistent tilt past roughly 8° reads as a drinking event.

Power3.7V LiPo over a 9V and a regulator.

Two options. A common 9V battery with a linear regulator is cheaper up front, but it takes more space and its voltage sags as it drains. A 3.7V LiPo is rechargeable, cheaper over its life, and takes far less room inside the housing.

I went with the LiPo. Size and rechargeability matter more here than the up-front cost, because this is meant to be something a person actually carries. The 3.7V feeds the ESP32's 5V terminal and the 3.3V rail powers the sensors.

The board carries the full chain that decision implies: USB-C in with 5.1kΩ CC pull-downs, USBLC6-2SC6 ESD protection, a TP4056 charger, DW01A and FS8205A for over-charge, over-discharge and over-current protection, and an AP2112K-3.3 regulator. A lithium cell in a bottle I carry around is not a place to leave out the protection circuitry.

Firmware and outputThe goal moves with the conditions.

The ESP32-C3-MINI was selected against five requirements: an ADC channel for the thermistor, WiFi, UART for the ultrasonic, I²C for the MPU-6050 and SHT40, and a small footprint. It meets all of them at around $11.

Notifications go to my phone through ntfy. The ESP32 posts to a server address and the app receives it: a morning hydration target, check-ins if I'm falling behind, and an evening summary.

The target itself moves. Temperature adjusts it by roughly ±1 fl oz per ±5°F over 30 minutes, and activity adds +1 fl oz per 15 minutes of movement. That is the whole point of the project, and it is the thing the phone apps could not do.

What I would do differentlyThe housing was harder than the circuit.

The electronics were not the bottleneck. The housing was, and I did not plan for that.

The design goal was a housing that threads between the body and lid of an Owala bottle, so it could be moved between bottles. The problem is that Owala uses a proprietary thread, the dimensions are not published anywhere, and I had never done serious CAD before. Reverse-engineering the thread meant learning to use calipers properly and learning to think about tolerance, and it took several iterations and more time than the entire circuit design.

The honest lesson is about sequencing. I ordered sensors before I understood how hard the housing would be, which meant the mechanical design had to work around parts that were already chosen. If I did it again I would design a platform the bottle sits on instead of a threaded collar. It's less elegant and less portable, but it works with any bottle, it's cheaper, and it doesn't depend on reverse-engineering somebody's threading.

That change also points at V2. The current design is welded to one specific bottle, which defeats the goal of using it every day. I'm rebuilding it as something smaller and bottle-agnostic, closer to a keychain counter, which means stripping it back to the sensors that actually earn their place rather than adding more.
